Recycling & waste in Australia · 2006–07 to 2025–26

Is Australia actually getting better at recycling?

In 2019 every Australian government signed up to a goal: by 2030, recover 80% of all the waste we throw away. Six years on, we are recovering more than ever. But the headline number hides which materials are doing the work, which states are falling behind, and where the rest of our recycling actually goes.

We're Not Improving Fast Enough

Australia recovered 49% of its waste in 2006–07 and 67.5% in 2024–25. Since the target was set, the rate has risen by about 0.8 points a year.

Extend that line to 2029–30 and we land at roughly 71%, about nine points short.

Building rubble (concrete, bricks, soil) makes up 35% of all waste and 86% of it is recovered. Take rubble out, and our recovery rate has crept up only about 3 points since 2018–19.

Dashed line: straight-line trend of 2018–19 to 2024–25, extended to 2029–30. It is a projection, not a forecast. Hover over points for exact values.

Plastics have sat between 10% and 15% recovered for the entire 19 years. No other material has stayed this low for this long.

Paper and cardboard are the surprise: recovery fell from 70% in 2006–07 to 55% today, with the steepest drop after 2018–19. That timing coincides with China's 2018 restrictions on imported recyclables (see section 04).

Glass has also slipped, from 59% to 54% since 2018–19.

Which Materials Improved?

 

Comparing 2018–19 (the year before the target) with 2024–25: only building rubble (+4 points) and hazardous waste (+8 points) clearly improved.

Paper and cardboard went backwards by 10 points, and glass by 5. These are the materials households handle every week, and they are moving the wrong way.

The national rate still rose because building rubble is so heavy: 27 million tonnes in 2024–25, almost twice the weight of paper, plastics, glass and metals combined (15 million tonnes).

Distance to the target

South Australia recovers 80% of its waste, the only state at the target. It was 85% in 2018–19.

Victoria and the ACT have gone backwards since the target was set.

The Northern Territory made the biggest jump (+19 points) but is still furthest away. Remote towns there rely on landfills, with few recycling sites nearby (see Recycling vs Landfill section).

Which Country Takes It

In 2017–18 China was the biggest buyer of our waste, taking 18.5% of exports. By 2021–22 its share had fallen to 2%.

Indonesia (26% in 2025–26) and Bangladesh (17%) took most of the slack.

Metals are 61% of everything exported and paper and cardboard another 23%. Switch to Plastics or Glass and the map almost empties.

Due to the ban of receiving recycled waste material such as plastic and glass in China during 2021, most of the exports were diverted to South East Asian Countries.

Bangladesh climbed from 8th to 2nd, and briefly 1st in 2023–24, almost entirely by buying Australian scrap metal (99.6% of its tonnes).

Recycling Sites Vs Landfills

Every circle is a recycling site and every grey triangle is a landfill. Filter by material, hide the landfills, or zoom to your state.

The 2022 national infrastructure database lists 1,269 landfills, but only 87 plastics, 65 paper and 22 glass reprocessing sites.

Recyclers cluster around Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane, Adelaide and Perth. Victoria hosts 39 of the 87 plastics reprocessors.

Across most of Western Australia, Queensland and the Northern Territory, the nearest option is a landfill. The NT has 117 landfills and 6 recycling sites. Distance helps explain why the NT and Queensland trail the map in the state comparison section.

These are counts of sites, not how much each one can process.

Conclusion

The headline is flattering

The rise to 67.5% is driven by building rubble. Without it, recovery has barely moved since 2018–19.

Household materials are slipping

Paper and glass recovery have fallen since the target was set, and plastics are stuck near 13%.

Exporting less means processing more here

Bans have cut plastic and glass exports, but recycling sites are few and concentrated in the big cities.

At the current pace Australia reaches about 71% by 2030. To reach the goal of 80%, we need to focus more on the materials that pass through our own bins, not just the ones that come off building sites.
